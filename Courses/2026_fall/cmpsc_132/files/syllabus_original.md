General Information

 

Prerequisite: CMPSC 131 - Programming and Computation I: Fundamentals. Students are expected to be fluent with the basic syntax of Python to take this course. 

Course Description: CMPSC 132 builds upon the foundations of programming and computation by introducing and studying the data structures and programming language features that support the design and construction of large-scale software systems. It introduces the foundations of object-oriented programming, the design and analysis of efficient algorithms using important data structures, and programming techniques that support reusable and modular program components, including data abstraction, polymorphism, and higher-order functions. This course builds upon the foundations of programming and computation by introducing and studying:

The data structures and programming language features that support the design and construction of large-scale software systems.
Data structures for the design and analysis of efficient algorithms
Object-oriented and functional programming in Python
Abstract data types
Elementary complexity theory
Searching and Sorting algorithms
Elementary programming language theory

Learning Objectives: By the end of this course students will be able to:

Design and implement computational solutions to problems in standard style.
Minimize and remove program errors.
Analyze the efficiency of algorithms. (“Big-O” run time analysis of algorithms)
Balance between metrics such as short running time and program conciseness.
Design and implement a software system composed of modular reusable software components.
Write, debug and predict the behavior of programs.

It is my practice not to round the numerical grade before mapping to letter grades by the table. This can be a sore point, so let me explain. For example, I use ≥90.00 as the transition from a B+ to an A-. This means that if your numerical grade is 89.9, I map it to a B+ and not an A-. It can be heartbreaking to miss a grade boundary by -0.1, I know. But to round up, say, every numerical grade ≥89.50 to 90.00 and map that to an A-, means that the transition from B+ to A- is actually 89.50, not 90.00; and that would mean that a grade of 89.4 would miss a grade boundary by -0.1. (It would also mean that me announcing the grade boundary of 90.00 in the syllabus is not accurate). No matter what policy is followed, some could miss a grade boundary by a hair. Even though there may be some psychological difference between the two situations, I prefer to keep it straightforward by announcing the sharp grade boundary and then following it strictly. I find it helps keep the process more objective, and does not allow room for subjective grade adjustments, which are almost always unfair. Since our policy is uniform consideration, I do not respond to individual requests for special consideration.

Course Activities

 

Module Checkpoints

These are weekly Canvas quizzes assigned to assess your understanding of the conceptual material presented in the lectures. Checkpoints should be completed as soon as the lectures and reading notes are completed. You will have two attempts for each checkpoint, and we will record the highest score from your two attempts. You will be able to see any incorrect answers only once after each attempt. There is a deadline for each checkpoint, we strongly suggest you stick to it.

The main goal of these activities is to help you stay on track with the weekly material, while also raise awareness on areas of struggle with the course material, so it is important that you answer these questions without the use of any online sources, after watching the assigned video-lectures and using only your notes and course materials. We are not interested on answers from other sources, if you don't know the answer to a question, use our Module Checkpoint Questions channel on Teams to receive guidance from the course staff and your classmates. The only way to earn a zero in these activities is by not submitting them.

 

Recitation Activities

These activities are meant to provide programming practice and conceptual review to complete labs and homework assignments. Each weekly activity will be completed in the computer lab and will be related to its previous week’s module, so students are expected to be familiar with the material covered the previous week. The course staff in charge of each recitation will be available to assist students to achieve the goal of the activity, however, students are also welcome to work with other students and revise the course material provided on Canvas. Recitation activities are graded on effort and number of tests passed (or number of fully correct questions). Don’t hesitate to ask us for help if you need assistance. Submissions will be done during the recitation time only, no submissions will be accepted outside the recitation you attended.

Attending recitation does not guarantee a full score for the recitation activity: no submission or submissions that do not show any effort to achieve the goal of the assignment (failing all cases) or that the student is not up-to-date with the lectures and readings receives a 0 score.

It is against the course academic integrity policy to submit recitation work on Canvas or Gradescope when you are not present in recitation. Violations of this policy will be reported as an academic integrity violation.

Assignments

Each module has problems assigned for you to work on, most of which will involve writing, debugging, and testing programs. These assignments come in two categories: lab exercises and homework assignments. All assignments will be collected electronically on the specified due date and time. Assignments turned in after the deadline will NOT be accepted unless an extension was previously granted by the instructor (for more details on extensions, please refer to our Late Submission Policy). Please do not email the instructor asking to take your submission, those requests will be ignored. Each module has a due date to complete all the assignments. Don’t wait until the last minute to complete your work!

You are encouraged to discuss the assignments with other students (high-level design issues, how to use our course tools, clarification of assignment instructions, etc.) using our CMPSC 132 - Fall 2026 Team Q&A Forum, as long as you write and submit your own code. The purpose of the assignments is for you to learn the course material, not to prove that you already know it. Therefore, you can expect to receive substantial assistance from the instructor and Teaching/Learning Assistants when you ask for help and come to office hours.

Writing your own code to attempt each function
Using descriptive variable names

Passing the doctests provided in the starter code

It is better to complete/show your best effort and turn in assignments before the deadline. All assignments must be completed on time to receive credit, unless prior permission has been given by the instructor.

However, everyone can encounter circumstances that prevent them from submitting assignments on time. Therefore, we will provide a grace period of 24 hours for all assignments that can be used to submit assignments up to 24 hours late with no penalty. You do not need to submit a request to use the grace period, it is available for all students (no need to email the instructor for approval). Note that grace periods may only be used on labs and homework assignments, and may not be used on module checkpoints, quizzes, recitation activities or anything else. There will never be a penalty for using the grace days, I understand that life happens and I want to ensure that all students are provided with the support and flexibility they need to succeed, regardless of personal circumstances; just keep in mind that open communication with your instructor is key!

 

Extension Policy

Due to the scale and pacing of the class, we cannot give individual extensions on assignments. However, there are a few exceptions and ALL extensions must be requested before the assignment deadline:

Medical Emergencies: if you are feeling sick, contact Student Health Services. Students with prolonged sickness or University-mandated isolation may obtain extensions at the discretion of the instructor, or by request from Student Health Services or any medical professional.  Students who have prolonged absences due to medical treatment should notify their academic advisor, since a medical emergency is likely to impact their academics more broadly. 
Personal/Family Emergencies: if you are having a family or personal emergency (such as a death in the family or a mental health crisis), reach out to your instructor immediately so we can set up an extension to support you in your time of need.
University-Related Absences: if you are attending a university-approved event (such as a multi-day athletic/academic trip organized by the university), you may request an extension for the duration of the trip. Note that we will consider most job interview trips to fall in this category. You must provide confirmation of your attendance, usually from a faculty or staff organizer of the event.
University-Related Event: if you are deeply involved in an event involving some university-related club or organization that you are in, where it will take nearly all of your time over multiple days, we will consider granting an extension on that week's homework. Please note: this is a one-time deal for the semester

We certainly understand that unfortunate things happen in life. However, not all unfortunate circumstances are valid reasons for an extension. Nearly all situations that make you run late on an assignment can be avoided with proper planning (often just starting early). For example:

"It's 11:58 PM and there is no internet": you can always submit multiple times before the deadline - it's not a good idea to wait for the deadline for your first submission.
"My computer crashed and I lost everything": Use could services such Google Drive, One Drive, Dropbox, or similar to do real-time backup - recover your files and finish your assignment in a computer lab or borrowed computer.

 

Regrading Policy

Exams: If you disagree with the grading, such as if you think your solution actually does work, or that your solution is more nearly correct than it was given credit for, request a regrade on Gradescope. All quiz regrade requests must be submitted on or before regrade request deadline, and final exam regrade requests within 24 hours of grade release. In your request you must include 1) Question number and 2) A brief (courteous/humble) explanation of what you think was misgraded and why. -- You will not be given credit if you use constructs that you were told not to use or if your solution is unrelated to the problem!

Assignments: If you feel that there is an error in the grading of an assignment, contact any of the Learning Assistants or attend office hours with a brief description of the error on or before regrade request deadline. Consider the information provided in the Assignments section and our Submission and Debugging Policy before requesting a regrade. If after talking with an assistant you are not satisfied with your grade, you may contact the instructor for further evaluation and total regrade of your assignment. 

Note that regrade requests will result in the entire problem being regraded, not just the possibly-incorrectly-graded part.  If we notice anywhere that you were mistakenly given too many points, we will also correct this, so it is possible that a regrade request will result in you receiving a lower mark than what you started with. Scores will not be reconsidered after one week, no exceptions.

 

Office Hours Policy

Be prepared when you go to get help from the course staff. You can expect to receive substantial assistance as long as you come prepared, this means, you have watched the lectures (or read the textbook/notes), read the instruction sheets, and thought of possible ideas to solve the problem (regardless of their correctness).

Sending an email with your code asking what is wrong? Or why is not working?, or requesting help without completing the lectures or without describing in your own words what task the function is supposed to solve, are not clear or helpful ways to ask for help. Remember that the we are here to guide you, not to complete your homework. Instead, walk us through your code: “I am doing X to get Y but I am getting W”. Use the Python Debugger (or any other debugger you prefer) before asking for help. Including the function call and trace back messages are helpful for us to assist you better.  

If you are unable to implement any part of the assignment, please outline your thought process in brief English sentences instead of code instead of coming with an empty file. The goal is to improve your problem-solving skills as we assist you to translate your ideas into code. If you request help on an empty file, you will be asked to write this outline first.  

Don't be afraid to come to office hours! We like getting to know as many of you as we can.

 

Internet use Policy

Online resources can be helpful to clarify concepts or read documentation. There is a huge difference between asking ‘How to use str.replace()‘ and ‘code for binary tree in Python’. Academic honesty is expected of all students. Any violation of academic integrity is taken extremely seriously, and all assignments must be the original work of the student. This means, you can only use the internet to learn more about a command, not to search for a solution, remember that plagiarism will not be tolerated in this class.

 

Collaboration Policy

Collaboration with other students in this CMPSC 132 class is an important learning method that helps students to create higher quality code and improve their learning. However, collaboration has a limit, and in this class that limit is sharing code. You are free to discuss the problems with others beforehand, but you must write your own solutions. If you are unsure if what you are doing is cheating, please clarify with the instructor or Learning Assistants. The following guidelines will help you understand the difference between collaboration and plagiarism:

Students may only collaborate with fellow students currently taking CMPSC 132 using our Teams forum, the LA’s and the instructor. Collaboration means high-level descriptions, assisting with debugging, explaining a concept, etc. You should not exchange code or write code for others. If you write your code on your own and then share that code with another student, academic integrity procedures will be filed for both students. Under no circumstances you can share your code (total or partial) with others.
Your submission must not be substantially similar to another student's submission. Collaboration at a reasonable level (general concepts) will not result in substantially similar code. Verbal description of your code given to another student, or helping other write code line by line is a violation of academic integrity.
Do not copy solutions from online sources such as Stack Overflow, Pastebin, Chegg or public repositories on GitHub. These solutions should not be used for ‘inspiration’ in your code. If you need assistance starting or debugging your code, you must contact the instructor or any of the Teaching/Learning Assistants.
Do not use code written by students in previous semesters.
You must complete this work entirely on your own. You may not write code for other students or use any online sites (e.g., Course Hero or Chegg), technologies (e.g., ChatGPT, language translators), tools, or sources that are prohibited. 
Do not post your solutions publicly during or after the semester.  When a solution is found online, we record it and use it to check for copying. By reporting online solutions, you help us keep the course fair for everyone.

In summary, students are expected to hand in their own work. The assignments and evaluations are structured to help you learn, which is why you are here. Rather than copying someone else's work, ask for help!!! Remember that you are not alone in this course. The instructor and course Assistants are here to help you succeed. If you invest the time to learn the material and complete the assignments, you will not need to copy any answers.

If you are helping another student, don't just tell them the answer; they will learn very little and run into trouble on exams. Instead, try to guide them toward discovering the solution on their own. Problem solving practice is the key to progress in computer science.
