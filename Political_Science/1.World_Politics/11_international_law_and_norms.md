Shocked by the suffering of wounded soldiers after the Battle of Solferino, fought in 1859 as part of the Italian wars of unification, Swiss social activist Henry Dunant began a campaign to reform and limit the conduct of states during wartime. The result of Dunant's efforts, and the efforts of many others mobilized to the cause, was the first Geneva Convention, on the treatment of wounded soldiers, adopted in 1864. This agreement was followed by a second convention, on the treatment of members of armed forces at sea, in 1906, and a third convention, on the treatment of prisoners of war, in 1929. These three agreements were revised in 1949 and joined by a fourth convention on the protection of civilians in wartime.

These treaties outlining the laws of war are typically referred to as the Geneva Conventions, and they form one of the most clearly articulated and respected bodies of international law in today's world. War between states might seem an unlikely interaction to be governed by such an international institution. At the same time that states are sending their soldiers into battle to kill their enemies and possibly die for their country, they generally follow international laws regulating how that killing is done and how prisoners and civilians are to be treated. The Geneva Conventions are monitored and supervised by the International Committee of the Red Cross, part of the International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement. The conventions have now been ratified by all 193 members of the United Nations (UN), firmly establishing their provisions as nearly universally acknowledged and respected international law.

The conventions, however, have not ended all atrocities, especially within countries. In 1994, Hutu extremists in Rwanda began a mass murder of ethnic Tutsis. Roughly 800,000 people are believed to have been killed within 100 days. The killings ended only when the Rwandan Patriotic Front, a militia of mostly Tutsi rebels who had originally started the civil war, reentered the conflict and seized control of the country.

The international community watched and condemned this unmistakable act of genocide but did little to stop it. In 1999, then UN secretary-general Kofi Annan challenged the international community to define its obligations to protect vulnerable peoples when their own governments either cannot or will not protect them. Taking the lead, Canada sponsored the International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty in

September 2000. In December 2001, the commission issued its report with the provocative title The Responsibility to Protect.¹ The report argued that the international community not only must prosecute crimes against humanity but also has a responsibility to protect at-risk populations through military means if necessary. This new obligation was quickly dubbed R2P, after the title of the report. In April 2006, the UN Security Council affirmed R2P, recognizing it as a new norm of international behavior.

Although hopes were high that R2P would help realize the lessons learned from Rwanda, it met quickly with resistance from certain members of the Security Council, reflecting a fundamental tension between state sovereignty and international law. Although R2P has been applied in a few cases, its potential to enable more effective international responses when states commit atrocities against their own citizens has so far been underwhelming. It has failed to prevent abuses against civilians during conflict in Libya and Syria, and it has not prevented what many call ongoing genocides against the Rohingya in Myanmar and the Uighurs in China.

The Geneva Conventions are international treaties in which states consent to treat armed personnel and civilians in humane ways. Though the conventions have had an important impact, they do not diminish the sovereignty of states, as the principle of sovereignty includes the right to freely enter into agreements that constrain one's actions. R2P, however, stands in stark contrast to this principle. This new international norm, championed by the UN with the consent of its member states, explicitly limits national sovereignty in cases of widespread human rights abuses and state-sponsored violence. This is important because it legitimizes the intervention of states in the internal affairs of other states whether or not the targets give their consent. The Geneva Conventions and R2P are both part of a growing collection of laws and norms, respectively, that are broadly referred to as global governance.

The puzzle of international law, though, is this: Why would sovereign states agree to be bound by international rules? This is especially curious in the case of norms like R2P, to which individual states have not necessarily agreed to bind themselves but on which they may be held to account. Why and how do states collectively own behavior? How are private actors, who are supposedly subject to states under the concept of sovereignty, able to alter state behavior?

## Thinking Analytically about International Law and Norms

International law and norms are institutions that seek to shape how states understand their interests and that constrain the many ways in which states interact. States create and abide by international law because it facilitates cooperation. Indeed, where we observe strong international laws, the benefits of cooperation to states are generally large enough that these laws are often self-enforcing, or in the interests of states to follow willingly. International law clarifies the obligations of states, defines violations that are subject to retaliation, and sometimes provides for independent tribunals to resolve disputes. Through these mechanisms, international law helps states to understand, meet, and manage their obligations to one another.

International norms affect world politics by changing how individuals and, in turn, states conceive of their interests and appropriate actions in their interactions with other states. Norms are standards of behavior that reflect a community's beliefs about what constitutes appropriate behavior for an actor in a specified condition. Norms affect interactions because they are understood by states to be right and morally correct rules of conduct.

As we will see, international NGOs, transnational networks, and private transnational actors are also important in global governance because they can bring attention to new issues, advocate for changes in law and norms, or facilitate cooperation between states by providing information or monitoring compliance. They can influence international laws and norms though advocacy, setting rules, and even helping write international law.

## What Is International Law?

International law is established by states through either custom or convention as a way to facilitate international cooperation or regulate international behavior. The Geneva Conventions are one example, but others abound. For instance, the World Trade Organization (WTO) sets rules on the exchange of goods and services and increasingly on investment and property rights; the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) governs the use of the oceans and seabed resources; and the Convention on the Prohibition of the Use, Stockpiling, Production, and Transfer of Anti-Personnel Mines and on Their Destruction, more simply known as the Ottawa Convention, outlaws the use and production of land mines and mandates the clearing of existing minefields. International human rights law, which we will examine in more detail in Chapter 12, intrudes deeply into national political practices. Precisely because the specific rules of international law matter, states often bargain intensely over these rules and their interpretation. Law does not mean the end of conflict between states but is itself the product of political interaction and struggle. Nonetheless, international law is valued and typically followed for the cooperation that it helps bring about.

First, international law is a body of rules linked together in a common logical structure. Law is not simply a list of rules. Rather, to stand as law, rules must be woven together by one or more unifying principles, such as sovereignty. In contemporary international law, sovereignty stands as the primary unifying principle. Even though sovereignty is often violated in practice, it still forms the foundation on which rules governing state and nonstate behavior become international law.

Second, to have the status of law, a body of rules must include both primary rules, which regulate behavior, and secondary rules, which regulate how primary rules are made. $^{3}$ Primary rules can define behavior that is prohibited (“Don’t do x”) or behavior that is required (“must do y”). The Ottawa Convention, for instance, explicitly prohibits the use of land mines, which is an example of a law prohibiting a specific behavior.

The same convention also requires certain behavior: it stipulates that states must destroy their stockpiles of land mines within four years of the treaty's coming into force, which finally happened on March 1, 1999, when Burkina Faso became the fortieth country to ratify the agreement.

To have the status of law, primary rules must be made in accordance with secondary rules. One can think of secondary rules as akin to a constitution or the rules about how rules are made. To be governed by the “rule of law” is thus to set and enforce primary rules in ways that are consistent with secondary rules. Primary rules enacted in compliance with existing secondary rules are given the status of law. In international law, the principle of sovereignty implies a secondary rule about how other rules are made: under sovereignty, only states can bind themselves to international law. In the case of the Ottawa Convention, its primary rules become law because it was negotiated and ratified by sovereign states as an international treaty.

As a body of rules, duly enacted under secondary rules, international law is distinct from international norms (taken up later in this chapter) and other international institutions. Norms are often singular rules, disconnected from any larger body of rules, and can contradict secondary rules, especially sovereignty. Thus, R2P is an emerging norm and not law, even though it was affirmed by the UN Security Council, because it explicitly violates the principle of sovereignty by calling for intervention into the internal affairs of states under some circumstances and because it has not been accepted as binding by the states most likely to be subject to those interventions.

## How Is International Law Made?

States establish and abide by international law, like any other institution, because it facilitates cooperation. A specific type of international law applies even amid armed conflict. International humanitarian law (also known as the laws of war) seeks to constrain states from the worst abuses during armed conflict. States may also bargain over international law, with some preferring certain rules over others. For instance, UNCLOS was enacted in 1982 after 9 years of hard negotiations, largely over rules on the exploitation of mineral resources on the ocean floor. It did not come into force until 12 years later, once it was finally ratified by the required 60 countries. Even though 165 countries have now joined the treaty, and it is widely acknowledged as international law governing the use of the seas, the United States was so opposed to some of the provisions that it has refused to sign the final document. Most of the time, however, it is the benefits of cooperation made possible by rules and adherence to those rules that lead states to create and abide by international laws. This includes cases of reciprocity, in which states abide by international law or norms because they are better off if other states do the same. The UN Charter's Article 2(4) prohibition on the use of force is an example. Although many states would be more powerful if they invaded and conquered their neighbors, the benefits of peace and stability that go along with respect for territorial integrity mean that compliance with this aspect of international law is high.

In international relations, there are two principal mechanisms for making international law. $^{4}$ The first is custom, or accepted practice. States carry out certain practices because they believe they are legally obligated to do so. Such customary international law usually develops slowly, over time, as states come to recognize some practices as appropriate and correct. The law of diplomatic immunity, whereby ambassadors and other embassy staff are exempt from the laws of their host nation, is a classic example. This practice became law by custom because it was in the interests of both home and host countries to continue to honor it. Diplomatic immunity permits embassy personnel to report on their host country, negotiate with host-country representatives, and generally promote the interests of their home country without fear of possibly politically motivated prosecutions for crimes invented by their host nation. It also prevents potential misunderstandings between countries over the activities of their diplomats from escalating into larger disputes. Countries have learned that rather than improving and punishing diplomats, it is best just to send them home if they violate national laws. Despite occasional abuses, such as diplomats racking up large numbers of parking tickets for traffic violations, the practice of diplomatic immunity is sufficiently beneficial to home and host countries that, over time, it came to be recognized as international law.

Secondary rules that govern the establishment of customary international law remain vague, however. The number of countries that must recognize a practice as legitimate and the length of time before it is accepted as law are not specified by any other rule. For this reason, as the examples of diplomatic immunity, freedom of the seas, and crimes against humanity show, customary international law is increasingly being codified in formal international agreements, particularly in areas where specific rules are contentious. But overall, customary international law tends to evolve in areas where states have strong incentives to cooperate and benefit little from violating the rules that support this cooperation.

International treaties, which are negotiated and then ratified by states, represent the second mechanism

for creating international law. International treaties typically originate in a convention to negotiate a new agreement involving a large number of states. It is not necessary for a treaty-drafting process to involve all states, and many important treaties were initiated by smaller groups of states. Some agreements are easily reached, such as the first Geneva Convention (though the original negotiations involved just 12 states). As in the UNCLOS negotiations, however, hard bargaining may ensue precisely because substantial gains are at stake and states seek deals more favorable to their interests.

Within global governance more generally, these two mechanisms for creating international law apply to both public international law—or the regulation of interactions among states or between states and other actors—and private international law, in which a separate body of law regulates private but transnational legal battles.

As we will explain in the next section, agreements differ in their obligations, precision, and delegation, sometimes in order to address conflicts of interests that would otherwise prevent states from reaching agreement. Once a convention is finalized by international negotiations, the agreement must then be ratified by each member state in accordance with its domestic constitutional provisions. By ratifying treaties like the Geneva Conventions, states voluntarily take on the constraints of international law, in many cases binding their own state to specific standards in the future. This type of state commitment to international law is not interpreted as a violation of the principle of sovereignty. With international conventions, the secondary rules of international law are quite clear: countries that sign an international convention are bound to respect the terms of the agreement.

## WHAT SHAPED OUR WORLD? Crimes against Humanity

The concept of crimes against humanity is one of the most significant developments in international law of the last century.

Institutions The Rome Statute of the ICC, adopted in 1998 and entered into force in 2002, defines crimes against humanity as any of the following acts when committed as part of a planned and systematic attack directed against any civilian population: (1) murder; (2) extermination; (3) enslavement; (4) deportation or forced population transfer; (5) imprisonment or other severe deprivation of liberty in violation of fundamental rules of international law; (6) torture; (7) rape and other forms of sexual violence; (8) persecution against any identifiable group based on political affiliation, race, nationality, culture, religion, or gender; (9) enforced disappearance of persons; and (10) apartheid. In contrast, war crimes are single, isolated instances of abuse carried out during armed conflict. Crimes against humanity are intentional, systematic violent acts against civilians.

Interests The states' interests in prosecuting crimes against humanity have evolved and grown over time. The first known use of the phrase crimes against humanity was in the U.S. Republican Party's 1860 platform, on which President Abraham Lincoln was elected; it described the reopening of the African slave trade as "a crime against humanity and a burning shame to our country and age." In May 1915, the Allied powers in World War I denounced the Armenian Genocide carried out by the Ottoman government as a "crime against humanity." The term, however, was first developed systematically in the London Charter of the International Military Tribunal, under which the Nuremberg trials of Nazi leaders were conducted after World War II.

A traditional understanding of war crimes did not allow for the prosecution of government officials for crimes carried out against their own citizens. The Holocaust, in which 6 million Jews were systematically exterminated by the Nazi regime, was not then illegal under international law. With an interest in punishing officials of the Nazi regime for the Holocaust and other atrocities, the drafters of the London Charter gave first real form to the doctrine of crimes against humanity.

The doctrine was carried over into the International Military Tribunal for the Far East and the so-called Tokyo trials, which prosecuted Japanese government officials for atrocities carried out during the war, especially the Nanking Massacre, in which hundreds of thousands of Chinese civilians and disarmed soldiers were murdered and tens of thousands of women were raped by the Japanese Imperial Army. As is clear from this history, the concept of crimes against humanity originated in the desires of the victors of World War II to punish the losing states for atrocities conducted in the course of the conflict and to prevent similar behaviors from recurring in the future.

Interactions Through the charters and military tribunals created after World War II, crimes against humanity became international law largely through custom and were formally codified only when the Rome Statute created the ICC. Government officials are now held responsible for widespread abuses of their own citizens, potentially subject to punishments for violations of basic rights and possibly even to armed intervention to halt abuses.

This relatively recent innovation in international law is important because, like human rights law more generally (see Chapter 12), it defines international law as resting on a common humanity that is superior to the policies of any state. This law clearly constrains the rights and, more important, the actions of states within the international community. R2P is an extension of the doctrine of crimes against humanity. It not only allows for the prosecution of such crimes after they have happened, such as at Nuremberg and Tokyo, but also calls on the international community to actively respond to stop the abuses as they are happening.

The ICC is not without controversy. Its track record reveals a disproportionate focus on African states and leaders, raising important questions about the potential for bias in investigations and prosecutions. Partly because the United States is not a state party to the Rome Statute, the ICC has limited authority to investigate its behavior, with the notable exception of cases in which a citizen of a nonmember country engages in criminal acts under ICC jurisdiction in an ICC member country. This provision allowed the ICC to open an investigation in 2020 into alleged crimes by the U.S. military and Central Intelligence Agency in Afghanistan. In early 2021, the ICC was officially investigating 13 situations of alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity, encompassing 29 cases in eight countries.

## Is All International Law the Same?

Even when states have a shared interest in establishing international law to facilitate cooperation, they must still agree on what form the law will take. International law varies across several dimensions. First, international law varies in obligation, or the degree to which agents are legally bound by an international rule. High-obligation rules “must be performed in good faith regardless of inconsistent provisions of domestic law” or changing state interests, subject to certain exceptions, such as self-defense or necessity. $^{5}$ High-obligation laws, such as the Geneva Conventions or laws prohibiting crimes against humanity, are unconditional and require reparations to the injured party if they are breached. Low-obligation laws are merely aspirational, urging states to live up to some standard of behavior. As we will explain in Chapter 12, much early human rights law took this form.

Even high-obligation laws are often contingent or contain escape clauses. The 1994 UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), which we will discuss in Chapter 13, requires parties to the agreement to reduce their greenhouse gas emissions, but only after they consider “their specific national and regional development priorities.” Likewise, the Limited Test Ban Treaty enacted in 1963, which outlaws certain forms of nuclear weapons testing, permits states to withdraw from the agreement if they decide that “extraordinary events” jeopardize their “supreme interests.”

A second dimension on which international law varies is in its precision, or the degree to which international legal obligations are fully specified. More precise laws narrow the scope for reasonable interpretation. An important aspect of precision is that “for a set of rules, precision implies not just that each rule in the set is unambiguous, but that rules are related to one another in a noncontradictory way, creating a framework within which case-by-case interpretation can be coherently carried out.” $^{6}$

Much of international law is quite precise, with international treaties running to hundreds and sometimes thousands of pages of definitions, terms, and rules. The UNCLOS treaty, for example, contains 17 parts, 320 articles, and 9 annexes, plus an additional agreement (with 9 more annexes) elaborating on Part XI of the treaty. States often seek to retain control over international law and to limit future interpretations of the law by being as precise as possible in the treaty itself.

Other agreements, however, are remarkably imprecise. Commercial treaties, for instance, typically require states to create “favorable conditions” for investment and to avoid “unreasonable regulations.” Such imprecise terms are usually not the product of poor legal draftsmanship but rather reflect continuing disagreement between states over the exact nature of the obligations being specified. Vague terms ensure that settling on an agreement will not bring bargaining to a close and that states will continue to negotiate, albeit within a legalized framework.

By considering these three dimensions—obligation, precision, and delegation—we can distinguish between hard and soft law. $^{7}$ Hard law is obligatory and precisely defined, and it delegates substantial authority to third parties, particularly international courts. Soft law is aspirational and ambiguous, and it does not delegate significant powers to third parties. Countries often adopt soft law because it is easier to achieve, is more flexible and therefore better suited to dealing with uncertain futures, infringes less on state sovereignty, and facilitates compromise. Soft law is not a failure of lawmaking but reflects intentional choices by states to write law appropriate to the issue area or to the extent of cooperation that they are currently prepared to accept.

Although there is no guarantee that soft law will lead to hard law, agreements in new issue areas often begin as soft law that sets goals for states and creates frameworks for continuing negotiations. This soft law may eventually become hard law as states gain experience, bargain over specific issues, and write more detailed treaties. ## Does International Law Matter?

Can international law “domesticate” international politics? That is, can international law become more like domestic law found within states? Can the use of force in international relations be tamed by international law? Perhaps no questions have vexed diplomats and scholars of international relations more.

Supporters of international law view it as an effective—perhaps essential—tool in facilitating cooperation and managing conflicts. According to international lawyer Louis Henkin, “almost all nations observe almost all principles of international law and almost all of their obligations almost all of the time.” $^{8}$ Sometimes high levels of compliance result from more or less explicit enforcement mechanisms in international law. Yet states may comply with international law even without enforcement provisions.

International lawyers Abram Chayes and Antonia Chayes argue that states typically do comply with international law, and when they fail to do so, it is either because the law itself is imprecise or because they lack the capacity to fulfill their obligations. $^{9}$ In their view, states want to observe, say, the Geneva Conventions on the rules of war, but they may not have effective control over their own soldiers, who may abuse prisoners. Such was the case in the infamous Abu Ghraib scandal that shocked the world in 2004, when pictures of American soldiers abusing Iraqi prisoners surfaced online. Proponents point to the high rates of compliance as evidence of international law's potential. In this view, law can be a significant constraint on the interactions of states.

More skeptical observers see international law as unrealistic and utopian, or at least a reflection of state interests, rather than as a severe constraint on state actions. Two main problems exist. First, law is seldom precise enough to deal with every possible interaction between actors. Indeed, in domestic politics, lawyers earn their livings from and courts are clogged with cases in which the parties disagree on what the law says or requires in their specific circumstances. We should hardly expect international law to be more precise and less open to creative interpretation than domestic law is. In one telling instance, when NATO was considering intervening in Kosovo in 1999, British foreign secretary Robin Cook told U.S. secretary of state Madeleine Albright that he had “problems with our lawyers” over using force without the approval of the UN Security Council. Albright responded that he should “get new lawyers.” $^{10}$ Albright’s sentiment still applies today. If international law is malleable and can be interpreted to justify whatever states might want to do, it is a weak constraint, at best, on their actions.

Second, as we have seen, international law is the product of states' interests and interactions. States decide the rules by which they will constrain themselves.

Even though an agreement may specify enforcement provisions, the enforcement of international law ultimately depends on the principle of national self-help. It is a truth of world politics that there is no central authority higher than the state that can enforce law between all states. As we discussed in Chapter 5, there are no police in international relations. At most, international law sets the terms and conditions under which victims can legally and legitimately retaliate against countries that violate rules of behavior.

Even in world trade, an area of international relations with strong and well-developed international laws, the WTO only permits victims of illegal trade barriers to retaliate by imposing punitive duties on goods purchased from the violator. The WTO cannot punish violators itself, nor can it mandate that other states punish violators. Each victimized country must, on its own, decide whether to seek and carry out retribution for clearly illegal acts. This dependence on self-help necessarily limits the effectiveness of international law.

At the same time, countries generally follow international law because of the very real and important benefits of the cooperation that it enables. Like all institutions, international law facilitates cooperation by setting more or less obligatory standards of behavior, helping to verify compliance by articulating more or less precise rules, lowering the costs of decision making by creating rules that do not need to be renegotiated for each interaction, and managing disputes.

To take one example, in analyzing the problem of restraints on violence during war, the political scientist James Morrow argues that cooperation depends on reciprocity. $^{14}$ A state is more likely to treat enemy prisoners of war (POWs) well if it expects its opponent to treat its captive soldiers well, and violations by one state are likely to provoke retaliation by another. The Geneva Conventions, especially when ratified by both parties, make clear that the states understand, accept, and are likely to follow international laws regulating the treatment of POWs. This makes it easier for states to presume that others will treat POWs humanely and for them to do the same for theirs, and it defines relatively clearly what constitutes a violation that could be legitimately punished through retaliation. By setting rules that help clarify expectations on both sides, the Geneva Conventions reinforce an equilibrium (treating POWs well as long as the other states treat POWs well) that might otherwise prove more fragile or tend to erode under the stress of war.

In facilitating cooperation, obligation and precision may be the most important dimensions of international law, because, as just explained, they set expectations for the behavior of others and reduce the range within which states can argue that the law does not apply to their interactions in a particular instance. ## What Are International Norms?

International norms are another type of institution that constrains states. Norms are shared standards of behavior for actors with a given identity, defined in terms of rights and obligations. They rest on a community's beliefs about what behavior is appropriate for an actor under some specified condition. Norms may be codified into international law, but norms as institutions can exist and be respected by members of a community even when they are not written down in formal agreements. In our everyday life, many of our interactions are regulated by norms that have nothing to do with law. You are not required by law to hold an elevator for others, for instance. But to intentionally—or even unintentionally—allow the doors to close on someone rushing to the elevator is regarded as rude, and you may even apologize for letting the door close, even though the now excluded passenger cannot hear you.

Similarly, and with greater political importance, norms of fairness—which are not required in any way by law but reflect deep-seated social understandings—shape political appeals and policies in many countries, although exactly what is “fair” is not always clear and has varied considerably over time. In this same way, many now recognize a nuclear “taboo” in international relations—a normative prohibition against the use of nuclear weapons—even though the norm is nowhere found in international law.

Like law, international norms shape the behavior of states in important ways. Norms affect the interactions of states because they are understood by people within those states to be rules of conduct that are right and appropriate. In other words, norms define what it is we should do and thereby shape our understanding of our interests. In our everyday lives, of course, individuals and countries do many things they should not. Norms are often violated. They are standards of behavior, not prohibitions or straitjackets. But even if we choose to violate norms, we must then excuse our behavior, justify it, or risk being ostracized by our community.

We can group most norms into three broad categories. The first category, which we refer to as constitutive norms, defines what makes an actor legitimate or appropriate under given circumstances. What it means to be a state, for instance, is defined largely by norms. $^{19}$ At the level of everyday diplomacy, all states have flags, national anthems, and—even in the poorest states—ministries of science and technology. No law requires this, but there is remarkable similarity in the form and practices of states in the world today. To show that they are indeed modern states, many countries also buy advanced military equipment for which they have little use and even less ability to maintain—most notably fighter jets, which appear to have enormous symbolic value. More broadly, norms shape what it means to be sovereign at any point in history, which includes evolving ideas about when states can legitimately intervene in one another's “sovereign” areas of control, as reflected today in the new norm of R2P.

The second category, procedural norms, defines how decisions involving multiple actors should be made. As such, procedural norms are analogous to secondary rules.

## Extrajudicial Execution and the War on Terror

A strategy borrowed from Israeli antiterrorist practices, these killings were introduced as part of the post-9/11 war on terrorism by President George W. Under international law and many domestic laws around the world, the use of lethal force outside of armed conflict is illegal unless it is used as a last resort against a specific and imminent threat. During armed conflict, the laws of war suggest that lethal force should only be used against those directly participating in the conflict (that is, against armed combatants). Thus, the debate over the legality and the normative appropriateness of the United States' targeted-killing policy centers on whether the war on terrorism is considered an active conflict, on the nature of the specific threats posed by targeted individuals, and on the viability of other means of capture or punishment, such as arrest.

This policy also raises important issues associated with the benefits of reciprocity in an era of rapidly changing military technology and prominent conflict with non-state actors. Can the United States expect other countries to abide by international law and norms when it exempts itself from the rules?

In January 2020, a U.S. air strike ordered by President Donald Trump killed Iranian general Qasem Soleimani, moving the targeted-killing policy into even more controversial territory: Soleimani was a military leader of a country with which the United States was not in active armed conflict. The United States argued that it was acting in self-defense to prevent an imminent attack, an international legal standard with a very narrow definition. According to a 2010 UN report, in order for a targeted killing in the name of self-defense to be legitimate, the act must be perpetrated "against a real and imminent threat when the necessity of that self-defense is instant, overwhelming, and leaving no choice of means, and no moment of deliberation."

## How Are International Norms Created?

For a principle or idea to become institutionalized as a norm, the standard of behavior it specifies must be accepted as right and appropriate by a sufficiently large proportion of any given population, although how large that proportion must be is not always clear. Norms change. Where once it was acceptable in some countries to enslave people, including the practice of buying and selling humans in an open public market, we now find the practice morally repugnant. Though many people around the world are still held in various forms of bondage, not least in forced labor and sex trafficking, these markets are now illicit, and individuals found guilty of human trafficking meet not only legal consequences but also stigma.

Although norms can feel as though they are fixed permanently, international history suggests that norms can change dramatically. What does this process look like? Some norms change through the work of committed activists, some grow out of periods of norm contestation, and some evolve through intrastate competition for insider status, prestige, or international benefits.

A principle or idea is not considered an international norm until a sufficiently large or influential group of people or state leaders decides that failing to abide by the norm is wrong or objectionable. These processes can be sudden or more drawn out. For example, it took decades of domestic and international activism to end the apartheid system of racial segregation in South Africa, and although most of the apartheid laws were repealed in 1991, societal and racial tensions remain today. Similarly, colonial-era efforts to “civilize” foreign people through imperial movements were widely viewed as normal international behavior by powerful states. The

## Do Norms Matter?

Norms constrain states and other actors in two principal ways: first, by redefining their interests, and second, by changing their interactions. In both cases, isolating the effects of norms on policy is difficult. To the extent that norms define how individuals and states perceive their interests, the selection problem identified in the preceding discussion of international law becomes even more acute. If norms shape interests, and states act on those interests, distinguishing the independent effect of norms on action is nearly impossible. Nonetheless, there is reason to believe that the effects of norms on state action may be considerable.

Norms define interests by shaping what actors believe is right and appropriate behavior under specific circumstances. Countries as a whole may respect norms out of concern for their reputations as honorable or norm-abiding states. Such a reputation may have intrinsic value. It may also be valued because it facilitates cooperation with other countries or allows countries that possess this reputation to avoid other costs. As individuals, leaders may seek for their own egotistic reasons to be seen as moral and “upstanding.” Other leaders may do the right thing simply because it is right. Even if leaders are cross-pressured by circumstances or by self-interested groups to transgress norms, they can be held to account by their own citizens, and especially by voters.

States are not the only actors bound by international norms. Nonstate actors, including multinational corporations and rebel groups, can also find themselves subject to international norms, targeted by transnational advocacy networks, or pressured by norm-supporting states. Consumer boycotts of corporations are one tactic for naming and shaming companies that violate international norms or law.

Following several failed attempts to establish global corporate social responsibility (CSR) standards in the 1970s, international norms related to global CSR took off in the late 1990s. Global CSR norms now include a number of internationally shared expectations among states and other global actors regarding the appropriate behavior of responsible multinational corporations. As shown in Figure 11.2, these expectations include the UN Global Compact, which outlines voluntary corporate commitments to “universal principles” in human rights, labor, the environment, and anti-corruption; and the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI), which was formed from an alliance of nonstate global actors and sets standards for corporations to engage in standardized reporting on their own economic, environmental, and social impacts, both positive and negative, as they relate to sustainable development.

## Why Do Global Actors Comply with International Norms and Law?

In many areas of international politics, states and nonstate actors can agree on laws, norms, and standards and can even bring them into formal existence through international bodies. This is true across diverse issue areas in international politics.

Compliance under Coordination Problems In some areas, compliance is straightforward for states and nonstate actors alike. When the benefits of cooperation are clear and when states simply need to agree on a standard in order to cooperate globally (like the coordination games introduced in the "Special Topic" appendix to Chapter 2), then compliance tends to be nearly universal once the norm or law has been set, and the agreement functions in mostly mundane fashion. For example, the Universal Postal Union, established in 1874, has nearly universal membership and is responsible for the Universal Postal Convention and the Postal Payment Services Agreement, which require that states make available a uniform flat rate to mail a letter to anywhere in the world and that they treat foreign and domestic mail equally. States are better off complying with these agreements, and defection occurs primarily in cases in which state capacity is very weak. (That is, states without functional governments tend to have trouble delivering foreign and domestic mail.) Norms also operate in this space without too much controversy. It is technically legal to mail a letter internationally with a postage stamp that is placed somewhere other than in the upper right-hand corner of an envelope, but because it significantly slows down the transmission of that letter and risks that it will be lost, most of us comply with the norm without thinking too much about it.

## private authority

*An expression of legitimate rulemaking by nonstate actors in international affairs, including the establishment of norms governing the behavior of private global actors such as multinational corporations and international NGOs.*

The International Organization for Standardization (ISO), created in 1947, is one of the more vitally important global organizations that few people have heard of, and it has the highest rates of norm compliance by states, multinational corporations, and other private actors. The ISO is a unique form of private governance that sets voluntary international standards (norms) in areas like manufacturing, food safety, and health care. Although it has consultative status with the United Nations, it is an independent nongovernmental organization, and its members are national standards bodies rather than countries. The first standard set by the ISO was in 1951 for “standard reference temperature for geometrical product specification and verification,” and the body has now set more than 23,000 standards on similarly technical topics covering thousands of aspects of technology and business, likely affecting many aspects of your everyday life. Incentives to defect from these standards are minimal, and the gains from compliance are significant.

The ISO is an example of what international relations scholars call private authority. Private authority (also called private governance) in international relations includes “sites or locations of authority that are neither states, state based, nor state-created.” $^{28}$ The GRI, which we referenced in Figure 11.2, is another example of private governance; though participation is voluntary, the increasing uptake by corporations suggests that avoiding the GRI can itself come with reputational costs. $^{29}$

Compliance under Collaboration Problems In areas of global governance plagued by compliance problems (see the discussion of collaboration problems in Chapter 2), a number of strategies for encouraging compliance and increasing cooperation cut across issue areas, types of global actors, and international law and norms. These strategies include providing information by monitoring compliance, naming and shaming violations, and pressuring more powerful states to reward compliance or penalize noncompliance.

If it is possible to violate a standard without being detected, noncompliance becomes more likely. Therefore, monitoring compliance with international laws and norms has become an important and influential strategy for increasing international actors' compliance. By revealing information about compliance, transnational monitors allow all parties to an agreement to have greater confidence that the other stakeholders will honor present and future agreements.

Having enacted a law, reached an agreement, or recognized a norm, global actors acquire information about compliance in one of three ways. First, they can rely on the self-reports of others. Many international agreements depend on this mechanism, requiring states to report on their efforts to reduce atmospheric emissions, for instance. This system is in fact relatively weak, as states that have incentives to cheat on agreements are also likely to have incentives to lie about their cheating. Self-reports are useful only when they can be verified by one of the other mechanisms.

In some cases, the technology associated with monitoring compliance with global standards can change the efficacy of the standards dramatically. For example, illegal logging in countries with poor regulatory environments was long thought to be an intractable problem, in part because once trees were cut and processed into wood products, tracing their origin was nearly impossible. Illegally harvested timber could evade even the most reputable certification standards, like those of the Forest Certification Council (FSC). Since 2011, however, new technologies have allowed organizations like the FSC to not only document supply chains but also audit corporate reporting by using samples of wood products to validate the real (as opposed to the reported) geographic origin of harvested wood products. It is now significantly more difficult for state or market actors to try to pass off illegal harvests as meeting environmental standards. $^{32}$

When noncompliant behavior is documented via monitoring, “naming and shaming” can play an important role in increasing compliance. Additionally, pressuring more powerful states or other influential international actors to unilaterally reward compliance or sanction noncompliance can also be effective. These strategies can be combined and used by TANs, global networks, formal international organizations, international NGOs, private governance structures, or even individual states.

States and their leaders typically value their reputations as “good” countries or individuals that respect and comply with standards of appropriate behavior. Multi-national corporations also devote significant time and attention to maintaining their reputations globally. When third parties call attention to violations of norms and laws, offending actors can be castigated or shamed into altering abhorrent behavior. States or organizations that are publicly shamed for violating norms and laws may become international pariahs that other states will be reluctant to trust, collaborate with, or include in mutually beneficial cooperative arrangements. Although naming and shaming might seem like a weak tool to leverage good compliance from otherwise strong states and powerful corporations, over the long run this strategy can severely weaken a state's reputation and put potentially profitable cooperation at risk.

## Social Media and the Arab Spring

Starting in Tunisia in December 2010, political protests rolled across North Africa and the Middle East. In a matter of months, as the protests spread, regimes were toppled in Tunisia, Libya, and Egypt. Meanwhile, the government in Bahrain cracked down on demonstrators, deterring further protests, and civil war broke out in Syria. Many of these events played out across social media platforms, including Facebook and Twitter, where protesters posted video, provided commentary, or simply communicated with friends and family. Indeed, social media were believed to be so central at the time that the Arab Spring was also called the "Twitter revolution." Yet, some have pushed back against the importance of social media as a political force, pointing out that "Twitter cannot stop a bullet." What role did social media play in the Arab Spring?

Zachary C. Steinert-Threlkeld examined the effect of tweets on protest activity in 16 countries during the Arab Spring.⁶ In any protest, there is safety in numbers—especially when the government is likely to target demonstrators. In considering whether to attend a protest, each person must anticipate whether others will attend as well, making protest a highly strategic interaction with other protesters and the state. Because the stakes are so high, individuals rely on many signals about the likely behavior of others. In the past, such signals included conversations with family and friends, declarations made in coffee shops, and encouragement from local religious or union leaders. Today, social media allow for broader communication in real time. Does wider communication make a difference?

## Conclusion: Can States Be Constrained?

*Figure source: Margaret E. Keck and Kathryn Sikkink, Activists beyond Borders: Advocacy Networks in International Politics (Ithaca, NY: Cornell University Press, 1998), 13.*

Can the behavior of influential global actors be changed by international law and norms? This remains a lively debate in which scholars do not have settled answers. On one side, traditionalists argue that international law binds states only to the extent that they want to be bound; in turn, other efforts to regulate behavior by nonstate actors exist only because states that might otherwise regulate, control, and limit these efforts permit them to flourish. From this perspective, international law and norms are at best soft constraints. Powerful states retain the ability to go it

<!-- PDF page 516 -->

alone, and they face few costs associated with noncompliance with international law. When globally dominant states become weaker, are replaced, or experience significant ideological change among their national leadership, the global international law and norms in place are likely to change as well. $^{34}$ In many ways the rise of authoritarian states like China and Russia, as well as the global consequences of the Trump-led U.S. withdrawal from international cooperation, provides an ongoing test of some of these arguments, as we will discuss in the concluding chapter to this book.

On the other side, globalists argue that international law, norms, and new areas of private governance are emerging as effective forms of global governance. In this view, political authority previously exercised by states is migrating to new supranational authorities that can regulate states through international law, voluntary standards, and external monitoring, in some ways relocating responsibilities formerly fulfilled by states. $^{35}$ Here, nations respect international law because they benefit from the cooperation it facilitates and incur costs if they don't comply. A variety of global actors, including global civil society, multinational corporations, and NGOs, can hold states and others accountable for their compliance. For the globalists, international law and norms are increasingly constraining states and nonstate actors in their actions both at home and with one another.

As with all debates over supranational authority, there are no easy answers. The persistence of international law and norms does appear to be changing patterns of state interaction in some issue areas, including human rights and the environment, which we will discuss in the following chapters. International law constrains some actions of some states at least some of the time, and it punishes or at least sullies the reputations of states that violate legitimate rules. TANs make cooperation between countries more likely by serving as endorsers and monitors of international agreements. They perform essential roles in supporting and observing compliance with both the international human rights and environmental standards. And global private governance continues to play an important role of communicating standards and raising the floor on acceptable corporate behavior.

Compliance with international law may also be influenced by what's at stake, though it is still debated whether compliance is more or less likely when the stakes are higher. Compliance is also likely to be affected by who would bear the costs of noncompliance at a domestic level—and by the associated domestic politics. ## Interests, Interactions, and Institutions in Context

- International law and norms are institutions that seek to shape how states understand their interests and to constrain the ways in which they interact.

• States create and abide by international law because of the cooperation it enables. Although states are typically dependent on “self-help” for enforcement, the benefits of cooperation to states are often large enough that international laws are self-enforcing, or in the interests of states to follow apparently willingly.

- International norms affect world politics by changing how individuals and, in turn, states conceive of their interests and by defining what constitutes appropriate interactions with other states.

- Private authority emerges in areas in which private actors are pressured by other global actors to comply with new standards of behavior. When other private actors begin complying with a new standard, norm, or law, the incentives for others to comply increase.
